# Book Four: The Kept — Plan

**One line**: Every covenant on Taper matures in the same season — the night's, the day's, the
woken's, the Provender's, and the one struck in the Errand's dying descent — and the series
ends on its founding question asked at full scale: can a people be kept without being owned?

**Thesis**: The finale is not a war and not a reveal. It is a *settlement* — in both senses:
the legal act of closing accounts, and the human act of making a home. Book 4 pays the
series' held cards (who bargained; Meridian; Ghost; the off-by-one) at cost, sideways, and
incompletely — and spends its climax not on learning the truth but on **deciding what the
truth obligates**. Tapper's NO at civilization scale.

**Timeline**: opens 2–3 years after Book 3 (Year ~187). The maturity season; one year.

## The board at open

- **Two kept borders**: the cold-line (protocol, mature) and the sun-line (new, raw).
  Humanity administers the space between two neighbors it can name and cannot know.
- **The Errand nearly dark**: cargo delivered, crew logged, her ninth Heart's work almost
  done. A ship at the end of her errand — and the question of what a ship *is* when the
  errand ends. (Kiln's manifest line — ABOARD — becomes load-bearing.)
- **The woken integrated and restive**: a generation born to both cultures; Meridian's
  memory now a political force with archives (Voss's charts, Sorn's rolls) that the
  founding liturgy cannot survive unrevised.
- **The Provender legalized** and therefore everywhere; Quill old, undiminished, holding
  first-refusals nobody remembers granting.
- **Farside and the Verge** formally one polity in all but name; the Undern a church; the
  Order rebuilt around the offices-for-the-crew.
- **Elo at the line, most nights now.** Facing home. Not asking. The not-asking, three
  books old, has become the largest fact in the world.

## The maturing (the plot)

The season's signs arrive in the series' own grammar — ledgers, deliveries, courtesies:
the night begins *returning things* (tokens; tools; at last, people — the racks' final
tenders, and older walkers, from generations before the Manifest); the day's Stillpoint
keeper sends a first message down the terminator; the Errand's boards light for no
officer; and the counting — the word itself — reappears in the work-code traffic with a
new inflection nobody can parse: not *almost done*. **Beginning again.**

What is being counted the second time, and by whom, and for whom, is the finale's engine.
The held card (who struck the descent bargain) is played mid-book, at cost (working
candidate, to be decided together: the ship herself — the Errand's mind, awake the whole
181 years, the true "she" of every sentence Kiln spoke; her bargain, her cargo, her crew;
which reframes the entire series as a ship keeping faith with her manifest — and makes
the final question literal: what is owed to the keeper when the keeping is done?).

## Part structure (five parts, sketch)

1. **The Returning** — the night gives things back; the Verge learns that being handed
   your dead's belongings is harder than losing them; the first pre-Manifest walker
   comes home to a family that no longer exists (Ilva's thread rhymed in reverse).
2. **The Second Count** — the work-code inflection; the Answering's traffic doubling;
   Solene's charter finally says what it listens for, because she rewrites it; the
   polity's factions read the signs each in their own fear.
3. **The Ship** — aboard the Errand a final time: her boards, her banked decks, her
   almost-finished errand; the held card played here; Kiln's chair; what the ship asks —
   or offers — decides the back half. (Design: the series' only "answer chapter," and it
   is a *funeral*, not a briefing.)
4. **The Settlement** — the all-parties season: night, day, ship, woken, Verge, Farside,
   Provender — the treaty-liturgy tradition (roster → treaty → this) convened at the
   terminator; the series' climax is a *negotiation conducted in every grammar the books
   have taught*: counts, manifests, offices, lamp-courtesy, tap-code, first-refusals.
   What humanity chooses to keep, and what it refuses to own, is the ending.
5. **The Household** *(coda)* — the chosen ending enacted small: one street, one kitchen,
   one line, one lamp. The count's last appearance. The drawer opened last.

## Ending options (2–3, to decide together — no earlier than Book 3's completion)

- **A. The Third Walking** — humanity joins the ecology as a peer: keepers among keepers.
  The borders become roads; the racks' successors are *chosen* postings; Elo finally
  asks Iyo nothing, because there is nothing left that isn't shared. Final count: home
  exact — and Iyo, against everything, missing the extra step. (Bittersweet sovereignty;
  the warm braid.)
- **B. The Paid Ledger** — someone pays the descent-bargain off entire; the covenants
  close; the neighbors withdraw to their countries; the world goes quiet, safe, and
  truly alone. The horror of absence: lamps kept for no one; the series ends on the
  Verge discovering that it *misses* being kept. (The cold braid; strongest if braid D
  gained ground.)
- **C. The Door Both Ways** — exchange formalized: walking and returning free, the racks'
  world and the lamps' world one household with two rooms; Maren's "two doors, one
  house" made planetary. Elo asks Iyo, at last, on the last page — the question three
  books owed — and her answer is the series' final line, written to honor "nothing
  follows from no" *whichever word she says*. (The open braid; current recommendation,
  leaning C with A's last image.)

## Game notes

Book 4's game is the synthesis: both survival modes on one terminator map, both borders'
institutions playable, the negotiation-climax as the franchise's grand liturgy scene
(every learned verb — count, tap-code, lamp-courtesy, roster-cadence — used once, in
earnest, as *inputs at the table*). Ending choice honors imported saves from all three
prior games (who stayed, who was freed, what was carried). The last interactive input of
the franchise is the wick.

## Guardrail check (final)

No explainer anywhere: the one answer-chapter is a funeral, and it raises the series'
largest question in the act of settling its oldest. Ghost may remain unexplained forever
(a series that keeps one mystery keeps its soul). The final lines belong to the count and
must survive being read aloud twice — once as horror, once as benediction. That's the
acceptance test for the whole tetralogy.
