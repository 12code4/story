# NIGHTWARD — Book One: The Walked

## Overview

### Premise

Taper is a tidally locked world circling a red ember of a sun. One face burns forever; one face has
never seen light. Life is possible only in the Verge — the ring of permanent twilight between them,
where geothermal vent fields bleed enough heat to farm, and where, 181 years ago, roughly fourteen
hundred survivors of the colony vessel **Errand** arrived on foot, having walked fourteen months out
of the night side where their ship came down.

Their descendants — about fifty thousand people in three hearth-cities — live by two fusion cores
("Hearts") the Founders dragged out of the wreck, and by the vents, and by an ethic of maintenance
so total it became a religion. The ship, and the six reserve Hearts sleeping in her hold, were left
where they fell: three thousand kilometers into the dark, at the end of a route no one has walked in
a hundred and forty years.

Two pressures end the long stalemate of staying:

1. **Heart Two is dying.** Lantern, the second city, has perhaps twenty years of power left, and its
   failure has begun making a sound the engineers can't parse and the faithful can't bear.
2. **The Walked are worsening.** For three years the sleepwalkers — people who rise in the night and
   walk toward the dark, calm and unstoppable — have been increasing. And this winter, for the first
   time, one of the lost came *back*: a man named Elo Weir, gone six years, found walking out of the
   night, cold as meat and politely alive, asking by name for the last living member of the ship's
   crew.

The Compact authorizes Relief Column One — "the Column" — to cross the night and bring back cores.
The story is the crossing.

### Genre dials

- **Sci-fi**: the load-bearing frame. Tidally locked world, generation-crash history, cryostasis,
  fusion cores, shipboard automatons, a thermovore ecology in the dark. The speculative elements are
  few and worked hard.
- **Horror**: intimate and invitational. The dark takes no one by force. It imitates, it waits, it
  asks — always through someone you grieve. Violence in the book is almost entirely human.
- **Adventure**: expedition structure. Regions with distinct character, waystations, set-pieces,
  factions, attrition, logistics-as-drama. The book earns its triumphs; the lights coming back on
  must feel like a victory even though nothing is solved.
- **Mindfuck**: experiential, present-tense, never explained. Perception divergence, post-storm
  discontinuities, a count that comes out wrong. Delivered through what characters (and later, the
  player) directly experience — never through exposition, never resolved by reveal in Book One.
- **Mystery**: **deliberately deferred.** See `06-deferred.md`. Book One runs no clue economy. The
  unknowns are ambient, like weather. We design the truth layer together, later, on purpose.

### Themes

- **Heat is love.** Everything warm was warmed by somebody. Maintenance is devotion; the Quiet
  Order literally prays with wrenches. The dark, whatever it is, is drawn to heat and to grief —
  the two things humans can't stop producing.
- **The cost of going and the cost of staying.** The Founders walked out; the Stayers didn't; both
  paid. Every character is somebody's version of this choice.
- **Grief is the door.** The dark reaches people through the ones they've lost. The book's horror
  and its tenderness are the same mechanism.
- **Personhood at the edges.** A 260-year-old man out of time, a returner who may not be the man
  who left, an automaton that has never made a choice, a stranger no two people see the same way.
- **You can be in relationship with a thing you don't understand.** The book ends not with an
  answer but with a changed relationship between the light and the dark — which is scarier.

### Tone rules (binding until we agree otherwise)

1. The dark never chases, grabs, or roars. It is never on camera as a *thing*. Its agents are
   people, and they are unfailingly gentle.
2. Wrongness is stated once, plainly, and not remarked on again. Nobody says "that's impossible"
   twice. Restraint is the whole trick.
3. Every supernatural-adjacent event obeys the private rule sheet (`06-deferred.md`, section
   "Rules of the weird"). No arbitrary spookiness.
4. Human evil (the Provender, the Rimwars, home politics) stays fully human — comprehensible,
   economic, banal. It is never revealed to be the dark's doing.
5. Sentiment is allowed. This is a book about people carrying a book of the names of the lost
   across a frozen sea. If a scene can be tender and horrifying at once, that's the target.
6. No third-act explainer. Nobody monologues the truth. Book One resolves the *journey* and the
   *people*, and changes the *situation* — it does not decode the world.

### Structure at a glance

Four parts, 26 chapters, roughly eleven months of story time. Interstitial "paper trail" documents
sit between chapters (logs, minutes, manifests, letters) — one per chapter, listed in each chapter
file. In the game these become collectibles; in the book they are the second voice.

| Part | Chapters | Region | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| I. The Verge | 1–6 | Hearthfall and the twilight band | Home, cast, stakes; the returner; the Column musters and departs |
| II. The Grey Reach | 7–12 | Ruins of the Founders' Walk | Archaeology of their own history; Provender; side quests; first losses; pursuit revealed |
| III. The Sea of Static | 13–18 | The frozen ocean | Isolation, storms, the pursuit catches up, the midpoint disaster, the stranger, first sight of impossible lights |
| IV. Night Country | 19–26 | Farside Landing and the wreck | The settlement that shouldn't exist; the ship; the racks; the gift; the price; the return; the coda |

Deaths, in order, and what each buys: **Pell** (ch 11, human violence — the Reach is dangerous
before it is strange), **Corun** (ch 12, walks — Iyo's first on-page failed recovery), **Old Say**
(ch 16, the ice — Brakes' spine breaks and reforms), **Cistern** (ch 20, walks *by choice* — faith
completes itself in the worst way), **Kiln** (ch 22, at the threshold of his ship — the book's
gentlest death). The Column leaves seventeen souls plus an automaton and a crawler; eleven are
alive at home by the end — four by early rotation at the Reachgate, seven the long way (six of the
Column, plus Auditor Vell with Frame's wardens).

### Why this premise (and the alternates on file)

Chosen because the geography *is* the theme (a literal gradient from light into dark), the
expedition structure maps one-to-one onto game acts/regions, and "salvage the cores" keeps the plot
adventure-shaped so the mystery layer can be designed later without load-bearing walls moving.

Alternates considered, kept on file in case we pivot: a drowned megastructure descent (vertical
zones, pressure as the resource); a generation ship still under way where the "world" is decks
(tighter, more claustrophobic, less adventure); a terraforming colony where the biosphere is the
haunt (more body horror, weaker geography). Say the word before we start prose and any of these can
take the same cast and themes.
