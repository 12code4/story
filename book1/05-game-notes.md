# Game Adaptation Notes

The book is written to be adapted, not translated: single POV = single camera; regions = acts;
side-quest chapters = optional content that is nevertheless where the characters get built. This
doc collects the hooks so writing decisions stay adaptation-friendly. Engine/scope decisions are
yours; nothing here assumes a genre beyond "third-person narrative adventure with survival systems."

## Structure mapping

| Book | Game |
|---|---|
| Part I (chs. 1–6) | Act 1 / tutorial region: Hearthfall + cold-line hub, mustering quests |
| Part II (chs. 7–12) | Act 2: the Grey Reach — hub-and-spoke (Saltcamp hub; Lighthouse 9, Cinder Row, Glass Fields spokes) |
| Part III (chs. 13–18) | Act 3: the Sea — linear survival traverse with set-pieces (Anchorage, storm, Lead) |
| Part IV (chs. 19–23) | Act 4: Farside hub + the Approach + the Errand (climax dungeon that must not play as a dungeon) |
| Chs. 24–26 | Act 5: the return — **regions revisited, state-changed** (see below) |

The return trip is compressed in the book but should be *played* in the game: the same map walked
homeward with changed world-state (safe passage, Provender's waived toll, storm-altered
landmarks, your own outbound campsites) — cheap content, maximal meaning. Backtracking as theme.

## Core systems (each is a theme wearing mechanics)

- **Heat is everything**: one master resource. Warmth = health regen, morale, social currency,
  crafting fuel. Fires draw cindermoths (beauty), spend supplies (tension), and — late game, once
  the player has internalized "fire = safety" — draw *attention* (dread). Inverting the campfire
  from safe-point to ambiguous is the game's single best horror lever.
- **The count**: recovery of walkers is the signature interaction — approach from the lit side,
  speak the name, take the left hand, and keep a rhythm minigame (breath/step cadence) while
  walking them back. Fail states are quiet: you lose the rhythm, they stop walking with you.
  In ch. 12's story beat the mechanic *itself* betrays the player (the count UI comes up wrong).
  Late game, the same verb becomes dialogue (Farside protocols).
- **The Manifest of the Missing = the quest log**: side quests are names. Families give you
  names + tokens at mustering; out in the world you can resolve names (found walking; found
  returned; found at the racks; never found). You cannot resolve them all. The endgame
  door-to-door (ch. 25) plays your own log back at you. This is the game's emotional bookkeeping.
- **Radio (Solene)**: diegetic interface for navigation, lore, and horror — tuning as a manual
  skill; the static-scape of the Sea; wrong-voice events scripted sparingly (never random). The
  no-static-where-static-must-be moments (chs. 20, 25) should be *felt* by ear before any
  character says a word.
- **Companion trust via rites, not gifts**: watches shared, repairs assisted, funerals attended,
  work-code learned (Tapper's tap-language as an actual learnable input glossary). Trust gates
  side chapters (Cinder Row requires Adder; the lens requires Brakes).
- **Post-storm discontinuities**: after each sky-tear, small world-state edits the game *never
  acknowledges* — a crate moved, a lost knife back in inventory, one extra bedroll by the fire.
  No UI callout, no codex entry. Players will gaslight themselves; forums will do the rest.
  (Ration these: three or four per playthrough, hand-authored.)
- **Returner dialogue rules as systemic literacy**: returners never lie, never answer *why*,
  only ever ask invitations. Teach it by exposure until the player can feel a wrong sentence
  coming. Elo is the flagship; the payoff is that by ch. 23 the player understands the formal
  question *grammatically* before understanding it emotionally.

## Set-pieces (one per act, already in the outline)

1. Act 1: a full recovery on the cold-line, played by rote, at tutorial pace (ch. 1).
2. Act 2: Cinder Row — a no-combat quest in a town whose dead keep schedule; the design target
   is "walking-sim inside a survival game and nobody asks for their money back" (ch. 10).
3. Act 3: the Anchorage cable-walk (traversal + standoff), then the storm in the pod (a
   one-room, all-cast pressure scene — the game's best writing per square meter) (chs. 14–15).
4. Act 4: the Errand — a climax location with no enemies: traffic, work, racks, a gift. The
   player's weapon wheel should feel embarrassing here. That's the point.
5. Act 5: Lantern relit — the one unambiguous triumph; spend the whole audiovisual budget once.

## The ending as player choice

Ch. 23's formal question becomes a real choice: any party member may stay (with authored
consequences at home in Act 5), and — for the brave — Iyo herself may stay (a short, devastating
alternate coda: the game continues as Brakes walking the line home... to be designed much later).
The canonical/book ending: everyone declines, Elo stays, Tapper chooses NO.

## Adaptation guardrails

- No bestiary. The Benthos gets one scene; the dark gets zero models. The horror budget is
  audio, light, and dialogue.
- No lore-dump collectibles about the *truth*; paper-trail items carry politics, logistics, and
  grief — the same discipline as the book (`06-deferred.md` applies to the game twice as hard).
- Ghost is prose-only weirdness: party barks disagree about him; the player model never changes.
  Resist the "each player sees a different model" gimmick — it centralizes what should stay
  peripheral.
- Human factions stay human. No Provender secret lab. Mother Quill's evil is a spreadsheet.
- The count UI is sacred: it may only lie twice in the whole game (chs. 12 and 26).
