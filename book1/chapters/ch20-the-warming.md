# Chapter 20 — The Warming

**Part IV: Night Country** · **Status: treatment** · Week twenty-four. Farside's festival.
**Side-quest-shaped chapter** — and the chapter where Cistern walks.

**Function**: One breath of ordinary life inside the impossible — the Column embedded in
Farside's great festival — so that the book's cruelest loss can arrive at its warmest moment,
in its gentlest form, and be *celebrated by half the people present*. The Warming is the
book's thesis feast (heat shared as sacrament) and its worst dinner party.

## Beats

1. **The Warming.** Farside's high festival, its logic explained by the doing of it:
   once a season the town banks every hearth but one, and the whole population — the
   living, the returned, the guests, the dogs — packs the Vein-house, the great
   terraced hall over the warm seam, and shares one heat for one night. (Home
   celebrates by keeping every lamp lit against the dark. Farside celebrates by
   pooling all the warmth in one room and letting the rest of the town go dark.
   Nobody on the page compares the two. The reader is trusted.) Reef-broth, bread,
   the town-band's machine-shop instruments, the chalk-tally game at hall scale,
   and — the Column realizes it one by one — everyone's coat is off. Five months of
   layered survival peeled in one warm room full of strangers who know all their
   names. The book gives them one uncomplicated page of it: the Column, dancing.
   Even Adder. Even, briefly, Frame. (Bank this warmth. The chapter spends it all.)
2. **The embeds.** The festival braided through with the party's threads, one beat
   each: **Brakes** with the town-band — their instruments are working tools tuned
   true, their repertoire is *machine-songs*, and she sits in on a shaft-driven
   hurdy-thing and finds the answer-line of ch. 15–16 sitting in their standard
   tuning, ordinary as a folk mode; she plays it; nobody in the band finds it
   remarkable; she laughs — actually laughs — for the first time since the Lead, and
   it costs the reader something. **Adder** and Reeve Stay trade soldiers'
   silence over cups: he offers Cinder Row (the whole of it, unprompted, plainly —
   his ch. 10 absolution has made confession his new discipline), and the Reeve
   receives it, nods at her pinned sleeve, and gives back exactly one line of her
   own wars: "Ours ended when we stopped winning." No more. (Parked, forever, per
   guardrails.) **Solene** in Mirin's radio shed, off the hall's edge: the town
   listener plays her the night band — and Hearthfall is there. Clean. No static —
   the evening numbers, a voice Solene knows from her mother's old rotation, arriving
   across an ocean of interference that ate her mast for months, **clear as a voice
   in the next room.** Mirin's shrug is total: "It's always like that here. Nights,
   especially." Solene checks the gear (correctly; it's mundane), checks the bearing
   (correct), and then does the bravest thing in her arc: she keys the town set and
   *hails home.* ...The band swallows it. Hearthfall, clear as family, cannot hear
   her. Reception without reciprocity — Farside can listen to the world it left, and
   never once speak into it. Mirin, gently, watching her face: "Now you know what the
   returned are for. Someone has to carry the answering." (Stated once. Left.)
3. **The turn.** Late in the feast, the town's bell — not the schedule: three slow
   strokes, and the hall's register changes without dimming: chairs turn, the band
   shifts to something processional, and the Reeve announces what the Column's hosts
   have known all festival and not said, because it is not their custom to say it
   early: *the Warming is also the walking night.* It is the town's door: those who
   are called go tonight, together, seen off warm from one hearth by everyone they
   love. This season there are two: a reef-farmer the town has clearly expected
   ("her mother walked at the last one"), and — the hall's faces turning, kind,
   congratulatory, toward the Column's table — **Nef Cistern**, standing, in his
   travel coat, his gasket-rosary wound quiet around his fist, his face at last —
   the book has been withholding him for two chapters for this — *unclouded*.
4. **The letter and the leave-taking.** No struggle scene; the book refuses the
   Column (and the reader) the mercy of a crisis. Cistern has been finished since
   the chapel-shed; the festival simply provided the door, and he uses it with the
   pastoral competence of his whole life: he has already written the letter (to the
   Column, for home — the paper trail; the book's single most load-bearing document:
   his statement that he is not lost, not taken, and not deceived — *"I have heard
   the congregation and I am going to it. Someone should say the offices for them.
   It was never going to be no one; I find it is not going to be me who refuses."*),
   already returned his medical kit to Frame's medic with case-notes annotated,
   already — this is the beat that breaks the room — taken each of the Column aside
   *during the festival*, one by one, for what they each thought at the time was
   just a good conversation. (The reader re-reads the chapter's warmth and finds
   the goodbyes hidden in it. Refinement note: write beat 2 so this lands.) The
   Column's protests collapse against his calm and the town's joy; Kiln, asked for
   orders by nobody, gives the only one he has standing to give — "Doctor. Walk
   well." — and salutes him. Ship's courtesy. The hall approves in the Farside
   register: congratulations, tears indistinguishable from festival tears, hands
   on his shoulders, bread pressed on him he will not eat.
5. **The walking.** The town walks its two to the dark-side gate, every lamp in
   Farside carried in procession for once — a river of light to the edge of the
   light — and the two go on alone, unhurried, the reef-glow taking them, the
   farmer's family waving *as at a wedding*, and the Column standing in the crowd
   doing the only thing their grammar still knows: Iyo counts his steps while she
   can see him (the count as farewell now — track the motif's migration), Adder at
   attention, Brakes' hands still, Frame — warden to the last — logging the time.
   Elo, beside Iyo, watches with what she finally, rules-compliant, identifies:
   not hunger, not triumph. *Collegial respect.* The chapter ends in the emptied
   hall, the one hearth banked, the Column's table with one chair pulled out and
   one bowl set — the town has already, without asking, begun keeping Cistern's
   place in the Farside way — and the last line belongs to the Manifest of the
   Missing, which Iyo opens and then closes without writing in, because for the
   first time in the book's vocabulary there is nothing true to enter: he is not
   missing. Everyone knows exactly where he is going. End.

## Character tracking

- **Cistern**: complete. Arc: orthodox → unhoused (chs. 10, 15, 16) → chapel-shed →
  *called*. The book never rules on whether he is right; his letter and Farside's joy
  vs. the Column's grief is the whole question, left standing. (He is glimpsed once
  more, ch. 21, at distance, on the Approach — walking, not waiting — and never
  again.)
- **Iyo**: counting-as-farewell; the unwritten Manifest line. Her category system is
  now fully bilingual (home's grief-grammar / Farside's location-grammar), which is
  exactly the equipment ch. 22 requires and ch. 25 will make her use on other
  people's families.
- **Brakes**: the laugh, the folk-mode answer-line — her thread of the weird is now
  *domesticated* (the town treats her hearing as a knack, not a haunting), which
  quietly reframes three chapters of her fear.
- **Solene**: reception-without-reciprocity + "carry the answering" — her instrument's
  whole meaning inverted; ch. 25's clean-signal coda roots here.
- **Frame**: logging the time — bureaucracy as the only bearable ritual; her medic
  inherits Cistern's kit (the columns' merger now includes care).
- **Kiln**: the salute. Also, noted small: he sat the festival out of the dancing,
  hands bad, and the town's returned brought *him* the warm stones, unasked, all
  night. The ship's people, tending the ship's officer. Nobody says it like that.
  (It is exactly that. Ch. 21 begins his last leg.)

has to carry the answering."

## Game notes

The festival is a full social-sim sequence (the game's one long no-combat chapter after
Cinder Row — deliberately rhymed: the dead town's forms / the living town's feast).
Beat 2's embeds are optional scenes the player walks between; Cistern's hidden
goodbyes are *findable* in each (a player who does all embeds gets his farewell to
them; a player who skips gets the room's shock cold — both correct experiences).
The walking procession: playable lamp-carrying, one input (hold the light), the
game's most expensive crowd scene. After this chapter the party UI keeps his slot
greyed-but-present for the rest of the game (never removed). Count-lie budget check:
the step-count during the walking is *true* — the UI grief here is that it just
ends at the reef-glow, mid-number, when Iyo loses sight of him.
